Background

Oral cavity and pharynx cancer is far more survivable when found before it spreads, yet most cases are found later.

The gap is one of timing, not treatment. A dentist can examine the mouth in minutes, but people who do not know their risk factors have little reason to ask. The term also covers two diseases: oral cavity cancer, driven by tobacco, alcohol and betel quid, and oropharyngeal cancer, driven largely by HPV-16 [3, 10]. One dental exam covers both.

Objective

Design a free, brief, private questionnaire that identifies which published risk factors a person carries, ranks them on a transparent scale derived from the literature, and turns the result into one action: raise it with a dentist.

It is a risk-assessment questionnaire, not a screening test. The screening test is the clinical exam; the tool’s job is to make that exam more likely.

Design

  • 12 questions, about two minutes, no account; English, Spanish and Portuguese.
  • Scored in the browser; answers are not stored.
  • Output is a tier and a next step, never a probability, which would need a model validated on outcomes.

How weights are set

weight = round(ln(OR) × k)
k = 4.47, chosen so daily tobacco, OR 6.0 (top of its 2.5–6.0 range [1]), scores 8

Summing log-odds treats risk factors as multiplying. Example: daily alcohol, OR 3.0 [2]: ln 3.0 × 4.47 = 4.91, so 5 points.

Interaction. Pooled INHANCE data found the joint effect of tobacco and alcohol greater than multiplicative, interaction parameter 2.15 [8]. round(ln 2.15 × 4.47) = +3 when both are current. The oral-cavity value, 3.09, would give 5, so 3 is the conservative choice.

Where the formula is not followed

  • Betel quid, current: 9, from the low end of its 7–10× range; the midpoint gives 10 [4].
  • HPV history: 5, not 6, because vaccination and history are behavioural proxies, not serostatus [3, 10].
  • Lip sun exposure: 2 and 1, half the formula’s 4 and 2, set by judgment because the OR is for lip cancer alone [11].
  • Not from an OR at all: dental-visit recency (detection proxy), “sex not stated” (population average), and the symptom answers (override).

Risk tiers

Cutoffs were set by judgment against these profiles, not derived. They stayed put when the maximum rose from 53 to 61, so no unchanged profile looks safer.

Clinical review

Reviewed by a board-certified oral and maxillofacial pathologist (August 2026) and a head-and-neck surgeon (September 2026). What changed:

  • Added the immune and radiation question [12, 13].
  • Added gum growth and tooth mobility to symptom guidance.
  • Added a caveat that many cancers occur with no conventional risk factor: a low score with a symptom still means go.
  • Stated the pooled-populations limitation and the provenance of the tier cutoffs.
  • Oral cavity vs oropharynx: results now name which site a person’s factors point to, and say when an HPV-driven profile is likely understated. A split score was rejected: it would rest on one question.

Limitations

  • Not validated against outcomes: no sensitivity, specificity or ROC. The score ranks; it is not a probability.
  • ORs come from different populations and are applied to anyone; transportability is untested and is the largest uncertainty.
  • One score covers two diseases; the HPV weight is a blended compromise.
  • Self-report: tobacco and alcohol are often under-reported.
  • The anchor OR, 6.0, sits above pooled estimates for current smoking and oral cavity cancer.

Next steps

  • Validation needs outcome data and a biostatistician; clinical review cannot establish it.
  • A prospective pilot in dental offices, pairing the questionnaire with the clinical exam.
  • Clinician re-review of the weights that depart from the formula, and of the tobacco anchor.
