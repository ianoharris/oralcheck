Hi Dr. Rawal,

I said I'd send you the final wording and weight before the systemic question went live, so here it is.

The question reads: "Have you had an organ or bone marrow transplant, radiation to the head and neck, or a condition that suppresses your immune system?" Three options, yes / not sure / no. That's your wording essentially unchanged, and I kept it as one combined question the way you suggested rather than splitting it into three.

The weight is 5, and I want to be upfront that this is the part I'd like you to push back on if you disagree. Every weight in the tool is round(ln(OR) x 4.47), anchored so that daily tobacco at OR 6.0 comes out at 8. For the systemic question I used a blended OR of 3.0 across the three exposures, which gives 5. That puts it joint fourth out of twelve, level with daily alcohol and with HPV history, and above family history and diet. It's high, but when you said weighted high I wasn't sure whether you meant "high" in that sense or something closer to the top of the scale.

My hesitation about going higher is that I couldn't find a single defensible pooled figure for oral cavity and pharynx specifically across all three exposures. Engels (JAMA 2011) and Grulich (Lancet 2007) both establish the direction clearly, and lip is dramatically elevated, but I didn't want to publish a precise odds ratio I couldn't point at in a paper. So I labelled the entry "2 to 4x (blended)" on the methodology page and noted openly that it's the least precisely derived weight in the instrument. If you think the real number is higher, or if you know a source that pins it down properly, I'd rather change it now than defend a conservative guess.

I also added a question on sex at birth in the same release, which you didn't raise but which was sitting on the page as a stated limitation. Men are diagnosed at about 2.6 times the rate of women in SEER, but I weighted it from an OR of 2.0 rather than 2.6 on the reasoning that some of that gap is tobacco and alcohol the tool already scores separately, and counting it twice would be wrong. That gives a weight of 3.

The other thing your review prompted was a fairly large rewrite of the methodology page. It now explains why each individual question is asked, including the two where I think the evidence is weakest (the HPV proxy and the dental visit question, which isn't a risk factor at all and is now labelled as a detection proxy rather than quietly sitting in the table). There's also a new section called "What this tool cannot tell you" that states in plain language that a low score isn't a clean bill of health, that roughly a quarter of oral cancers occur without any of these factors, and that the score isn't a probability. Your point about the no-conventional-risk-factor cases is what made me realise that needed to be said outright rather than implied.

The page carries a last-reviewed date of August 2026 and a line saying the revision followed external review by a practising oral and maxillofacial pathologist. There's no name and no institution on it, and there won't be unless you tell me you're comfortable with it and Marquette is fine with it. I'd rather leave it anonymous indefinitely than put you in an awkward position, so please don't feel any pressure there.

Everything above is at oralcheck.org/methods if you'd like to see it in context.
